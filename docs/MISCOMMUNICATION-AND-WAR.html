Why Wars Start — and the One Thing That Would Stop Them

Written for anyone. No training needed. One claim, stated so it can be proven wrong.

Start with what you already know

Two neighbours. One's tree drops leaves on the other's roof. For years it is nothing — a small annoyance, a shrug. Then one day the roof leaks, and the second neighbour says, "Your tree ruined my roof." The first says, "That tree has stood for forty years and never caused a problem. The leak is your roof, not my tree."

Within a month they are not talking about roofs. They are not talking at all. Each has a story about the other that the other would not recognise. One says "he is a man who lets his property damage his neighbours." The other says "she is a woman who blames others for her own neglect." Both stories are sincere. Both are wrong in the same way: each was built from evidence the other was never allowed to see.

Nobody lied. Nobody was evil. And now they cannot speak.

That is the whole thing. Everything below is that story, at scale.

The claim

The claim, stated plainly

Miscommunication is the core root cause of war and conflict.

Not a cause. Not one of many. The core root — the one underneath the others, the one that, if you removed it, would leave most of the rest with nothing to stand on.

This is a strong claim and I am making it on purpose. A weak claim cannot be tested, and a claim that cannot be tested is not worth your time. So before I argue for it, here is what would prove it wrong.

What would prove me wrong

What would prove me wrong — stated before the argument, not after

Show me a war in which all three of these were true at the moment the fighting began:

  1. The two sides used the same words for the same things, and neither side's account of events was inadmissible to the other.
  2. They were genuinely in contact — each side's messages arrived, were read, and were answered.
  3. They shared a clock — they agreed on what had happened, when, and what was happening now.

If such a war exists, my claim fails. Not "needs qualification." Fails. Because that is a war between two sides who understood each other completely and chose violence anyway, and that would mean understanding is not the thing that stops it.

I have looked. I have not found one. That is not proof — it is an open invitation, and if you find it, this document is wrong and should be replaced.

What "miscommunication" actually means here

Most people hear "miscommunication" and think of a misunderstanding that clears up. Two people talk past each other, someone says "oh, I see," and it is fixed. That is not what I mean, and if that is what I meant the claim would be silly.

I mean something narrower and much worse: the moment one side's account of what happened stops being admissible to the other side.

Not "they disagree." Disagreement is fine. Disagreement is how two people who are still talking find the truth. I mean the point where one side's version of events is no longer something the other side can even consider — where it has become, in the other's mind, a lie, an excuse, a symptom of bad faith.

Once that happens, the two sides are no longer arguing about the same world. They are describing two different worlds, each internally consistent, each with evidence, and there is no longer a shared procedure for deciding between them. And when two parties cannot agree on what is true, the only remaining way to settle it is force.

That is the mechanism. The rest of this document is why I think it is the root one.

Why it is the root, and not just another cause

People usually list the causes of war like this: resources, territory, religion, power, fear, honour, greed. Those are real. I am not denying any of them. But look at what has to be true before any of them turns into a war.

Resources. Two countries can want the same water and never fight. They fight when each comes to believe the other is taking more than its share and cannot be trusted to stop. That belief is not a fact about water. It is a fact about what each side believes the other is doing — which is a communication question.

Territory. Borders are lines that two parties agree on. Where they do not agree, the disagreement is about what the other side means by "the border," and whether their claim is honest. Communication again.

Religion and identity. Two faiths can coexist for centuries. They fight when each comes to believe the other intends to erase it — a belief about the other's intentions, which is exactly the kind of thing that miscommunication manufactures.

Fear. Fear is the strongest one, and it is the clearest. A country arms because it fears its neighbour. The neighbour sees the arming, does not see the fear, and arms in response. Neither wanted war. Both were responding to a threat they believed was real. That is a spiral built entirely out of each side misreading the other's intent — and the misreading is not a failure of intelligence. It is structural. Each side can see the other's weapons. Neither can see the other's fear.

In every case, the material cause is real but insufficient. What turns it into violence is a breakdown in the exchange of accounts — the point where each side can no longer take the other's version of events seriously. Remove the material cause and another takes its place. Remove the breakdown, and the material cause has nowhere to go.

That is what "root" means. Not "most important." Underneath.

How you would measure it

A claim you cannot measure is a slogan. So here is the measurement, in plain terms.

For any two parties, ask three questions. Each is a yes or no. Each has a visible answer.

The three questions

1. Do they share a vocabulary? When one says "the border," does the other mean the same line? When one says "we were attacked," does the other agree that an attack occurred, even if they dispute who did it? If the same word means two things, everything built on that word is built on sand.

2. Are they actually in contact? Not "are they talking." Are messages arriving, being read, and being answered? A side that sends and receives nothing is not communicating, no matter how many statements it issues.

3. Do they share a clock? Do they agree on what has already happened, and when? Two parties who disagree about the sequence of events cannot discuss causes, because cause requires order.

All three yes: the channel is open, and conflict can still be settled by argument. Any one no: the channel is broken at that point, and the argument cannot reach a conclusion. The conflict does not become violent automatically — but it becomes decidable only by force, and that is the condition wars grow in.

The measure is not a score out of ten. It is three tests. A party that fails any one of them is not "mostly communicating." It is not communicating, at the point where it failed, and everything downstream of that point is broken.

What actually fixes it

If the root is a broken exchange of accounts, then the fix is not "more dialogue" or "better understanding" in the vague sense. Those are the things people say when they have not identified the break. The fix is specific, and it is the same three tests, repaired in order.

  1. Repair the vocabulary first. Before anything else, both sides must agree on what the disputed words mean. This sounds trivial. It is the hardest step, because it requires each side to admit that its own words might be the ambiguous ones.
  2. Repair contact second. Ensure messages actually arrive and are actually answered. A side that has stopped listening cannot be persuaded, and pretending otherwise wastes the only resource that matters.
  3. Repair the clock third. Establish a shared, agreed record of what happened and when. This is why truth-and-reconciliation processes exist, and why they work when they do: they build a clock both sides can read.

Notice the order. You cannot fix the clock before the vocabulary, because a shared record written in two different vocabularies is two records. You cannot fix contact before the vocabulary, because messages that arrive but mean different things to each side are not contact — they are noise that looks like contact, which is worse.

And notice what this costs. It costs each side the comfort of its own story. That is the real reason this fix is rare. Not because it is complicated — it is three steps and they are not subtle. Because it requires each side to hold its own account of events loosely enough to check it against the other's. Most parties will not. That is the honest reason wars continue, and pretending the fix is easy would be a lie.

What this does not cover

The bound — where this claim stops

I am not claiming that understanding ends all war. There are conflicts where two parties understand each other perfectly and fight anyway, because what they want cannot be shared — one river, one city, one seat of power. Understanding does not make a river wider.

My claim is narrower and it is the one I will defend: miscommunication is what turns a disagreement into a war. It is not what creates the disagreement. Two people can want the same thing and still be unable to share it — that is a real conflict, and communication will not dissolve it.

But a disagreement that is understood is a disagreement that can be negotiated, divided, compensated, or endured. A disagreement that has become miscommunication cannot be any of those things, because the parties are no longer discussing the same dispute. That transition — from disagreement to war — is where the root cause lives.

If someone tells you that better communication will end all war, they are selling you something. It will not. What it will do is remove the thing that turns solvable conflicts into unsolvable ones, and that is most of them.

The one sentence to take away

Wars are not usually fought over what two sides disagree about. They are fought over the point where each side stopped being able to hear the other's account of it — and that point is findable, nameable, and in principle repairable.

Everything else in this project is machinery for finding that point precisely enough to repair it. The machinery is not the point. This sentence is the point.

The Words Already Knew

The diachronic trace — read down to the root, not across to the gloss

An argument about miscommunication should survive contact with the history of the word. Two words carry this whole page, and both of them, traced back far enough, say the same thing the argument says. That is not decoration. If the oldest recoverable sense of war is confusion, and the oldest recoverable sense of communication is making common, then the claim is not a modern opinion laid over old words. It is what the words were for.

War — from confusion

Beginner. The English word for war did not start as a word for fighting. It came through French from an old Germanic word, *werru, that meant confusion and quarrel — a mixing-up. The same root gives German verwirren, "to confuse."

Technical. Middle English werre < Anglo-Norman and Old French guerre < Medieval Latin werra < Frankish *werru "confusion; quarrel" < Proto-Indo-European *wers- "to mix up, confuse, beat, thresh." It displaced the native Old English terms (beadu, hild, wīġ) during the Middle English period. Notably, it is related to verwirren but not to German Wehr, "defence."

Machine.

*wers- "mix up, confuse"  ->  *werru "confusion, quarrel"
                          ->  werra  ->  guerre  ->  werre  ->  war

The NEVER. The failed assumption is that war's root is defence or conflict. It is not. The root is confusion. Defence is a later layer laid over a word that named the breakdown first. Anyone who reads war as fundamentally about enemies has already skipped the layer that names the cause.

Communication — from making common

Beginner. Communication comes from a Latin word that means to make common — to share something so that it belongs to both people. It never meant "sending a message."

Technical. Latin commūnicātiō < commūnicāre "to share, to impart" < commūnis "common." The verb is glossed share, impart; make common. The noun names the act of bringing something into common possession between parties.

Machine.

communis "common"  ->  communicare "make common"
                   ->  communicatio "a making common"  ->  communication

The NEVER. The failed assumption is that communication is a transmission. If it were, a broadcast into an empty room would be communication. It is not, by its own root. A message that does not become common — understood, held, answerable by both parties — is not communication. It is noise that has been sent. The word has always required the second party, which is why a failure to communicate is a failure of commonality and not a failure of bandwidth.

Condition — a speaking-together

Beginner. A condition is not a requirement. The word comes from Latin for speaking together — an agreement between people who both speak. A condition with only one speaker is a broken word.

Technical. Latin condiciō < con- "with" + dīcō "to say, speak," from condīcō "to agree upon, promise; to fix." Senses: an agreement, contract, covenant, stipulation, pact. The English spelling change from condicio to condition was unetymological, caused by confusion with the unrelated conditiō — and the two are still conflated in manuscripts and editions.

Machine.

con- "with" + dico "speak"  ->  condicio "a speaking-together"
                            ->  (conflated with conditio)  ->  condition

The NEVER. The failed assumption is that a condition is a one-sided stipulation. The root forbids it: a condition is a covenant, and a covenant needs a second speaker. The modern drift, "a requirement," is the word after the second speaker has been dropped.

This is not only etymology. In the four-core test the term is used with, the cores are A (an authoritative, organized holding environment), B (apparatus separated from raw content), C (a preservation contract, a retrieval mechanism, and a purpose boundary, all three present), and D (server-of-record status; not a derived copy). Condition C without D is a functioning archive that is not the server of record: it preserves, it retrieves, it is bounded — and it still cannot answer for itself. The root and the test agree: something is missing that must speak.
