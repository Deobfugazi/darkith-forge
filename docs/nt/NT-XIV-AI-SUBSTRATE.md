## §A — EDITORIAL ADMISSION

This Omega edition consolidates the surviving content of the three recorded NT XIV execution passes into a single canonical manuscript. Nothing has been invented to fill a gap; no missing source has been promoted to a reference; no open coordinate has been silently resolved.

The governing distinction is binding: a **chronology** records that something happened; a **causal reconstruction** establishes what made it possible, what selected it, what it displaced, and what became dependent on it. NT XIV is the second thing, not the first.

NT XIV's equations and closure rules are inherited analytical instruments from NT I–XIII, not automatically validated physical laws. They govern the manuscript's accounting discipline; their empirical meaning must be established before they are treated as measured quantities. An exact zero in a formal ledger does not by itself prove that the historical reconstruction is complete.

Two closure states are distinguished and must never be conflated:

- **Procedural closure:** every claim, transition, uncertainty, and missing coordinate has been recorded; the delivery gate has run.
- **Epistemic closure:** every required coordinate is resolved to the evidentiary standard; no open gaps remain.

NT XIV reaches procedural closure in this edition. Epistemic closure is not earned and is not claimed.

**Claim flags**

- RESOLVED — supported by an identified source or directly observable record.
- EXPOSABLE — source or mechanism identified, but full evidentiary detail not reproduced or independently verified here.
- INFERRED — the reconstruction's own causal synthesis; open to falsification.
- OPEN — the coordinate remains a registered gap.

A flag applies to a claim, not to an entire chapter. A transition can legitimately contain claims with different flags. A single status cannot honestly certify every coordinate in a complex historical event.

**Evidence domains.** The reconstruction draws on three domains: (1) the documented technological and institutional record; (2) the material and economic infrastructure beneath that record; (3) the generating model instance's observable behavior as an operating node of the substrate.

## §B — CENTRAL FINDING

The AI substrate is not a single invention, a single institution, or a self-contained algorithm. It is a historically selected and materially coupled system formed by formal logic, computation, statistics, neural learning, data production, accelerator hardware, research funding, corporate capital, human labor, energy, networks, and geopolitical controls.

The modern frontier regime emerged when several previously separate constraints became jointly tractable: large digital corpora could be assembled; high-throughput parallel computation became accessible; neural architectures could exploit that computation; optimization and distributed training systems could scale; and institutions could finance and operate infrastructure at a scale unavailable to most academic groups.

This is a causal synthesis, not a claim that scale alone explains capability. Architecture, data quality, optimization, post-training, evaluation, and deployment context remain distinct causal variables.

A central forensic fact of the present state: the 2026 Stanford AI Index reports that industry produced more than 90% of notable AI models in 2025 while disclosure of training code, dataset sizes, and training duration declined for several major frontier systems [R21]. The substrate has become more industrially concentrated while some of its most consequential internal coordinates have become less publicly observable.

## CHAPTER 1 — THE PRIOR STATE: BEFORE THE AI SUBSTRATE

The substrate did not begin when the field acquired a name. Before the name, at least four interacting lines of development existed separately: formal computation (what procedures can be mechanically executed); symbolic reasoning (representing and manipulating propositions); neural modeling (mathematical description of nervous activity and learning); and information and control (signals, communication, feedback). The missing condition was not an idea called intelligence; it was an integrated technical and institutional arrangement able to turn these ideas into sustained, experimentally testable machine systems.

The prior state was not empty. It was fragmented.

**T1.1 — FORMAL COMPUTATION BECOMES A MATERIAL POSSIBILITY**

- PRIOR STATE: Mathematical logic and theories of computation existed as intellectual systems; programmable electronic computing was not yet a widely available industrial substrate.
- CONSTRAINT: How could a formally specified procedure be executed by a physical machine rather than remaining a mathematical description?
- AGENT: Mathematicians, logicians, electrical engineers, early computer designers, and supporting institutions.
- INHERITANCE: Boolean logic, mathematical analysis, algorithms, switching circuits, formal descriptions of procedures.
- RESOURCES: Mathematical labor, mechanical and electrical engineering, switching components, laboratory facilities, early electronic hardware, institutional funding.
- FAILED PATHS: Mechanical and analog approaches could execute particular operations but lacked the flexibility, precision, and general-purpose programmability of stored-program electronic machines. No complete inventory of abandoned designs is established here.
- MECHANISM: Programmable electronic computation made formal procedures executable and repeatable at machine speed; a procedure could be reused across many tasks rather than rebuilt per task.
- SELECTION: Scientific, commercial, and military demand for calculation, code-breaking, simulation, and information processing.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Programmable computers, programming languages, operating systems, and computing institutions.
- VULNERABILITIES: Dependence on physical hardware, electrical power, programming correctness, and access to scarce machines.
- DIVERGENCE: The popular account begins with "AI"; the operational substrate begins with the general computing stack that made AI experiments executable.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the broad transition; EXPOSABLE for machine-level engineering, institutional financing, and the comparative record of displaced approaches.
- FALSIFIER: Evidence that early AI research programs did not depend on programmable digital computation.

**Sources.** [RESOLVED] The Stanford AI100 account explicitly notes that ideas underpinning AI — probability, logic, formal computation, electronic computers — preceded the 1956 Dartmouth workshop, and that Turing's ideas preceded the computing resources needed to implement them [R01]. ## CHAPTER 2 — THE SYMBOLIC LINEAGE: LOGIC, COMPUTATION, AND MECHANIZED THOUGHT

The symbolic lineage supplied a central proposition: aspects of reasoning could be represented through formal symbols and manipulated according to explicit rules. It made intelligence research legible as an engineering problem: define representations, specify operations, evaluate results. But formal manipulability is not equivalent to general intelligence; the gap between a formal representation and the world it represents became one of the persistent seams of AI.

**T2.1 — SYMBOLIC REASONING BECOMES COMPUTATIONAL PRACTICE**

- PRIOR STATE: Formal logic existed as human-executed intellectual procedure, not yet unified with a general operational model of machine computation.
- CONSTRAINT: How could symbolic reasoning be mechanized so a machine could perform problem-solving without a human executing every step?
- AGENT: Boole, Frege, Turing, Shannon, McCarthy, Newell, Simon, and contributors to mathematical logic and computation.
- INHERITANCE: Boolean algebra, formal notation, Turing's universal machine, Shannon's connection between switching circuits and Boolean logic, statistical inference.
- RESOURCES: Mathematical research, university institutions, early computing laboratories, programming languages, published papers.
- FAILED PATHS: The assumption that every mathematical or logical problem admits a practical mechanical solution; early programs faltered when formal representations failed to capture real-world context. A program-by-program record of these failures is not established here.
- MECHANISM: Construction of programs that manipulated symbolic representations for theorem proving and problem solving, on a substrate of formal logic made executable.
- SELECTION: Symbolic operations could be explicitly represented, inspected, and evaluated — early institutional prominence that was not proof of permanent dominance.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Programming languages, symbolic knowledge representations, theorem-proving methods, AI research institutions.
- VULNERABILITIES: Dependence on explicit representations, difficulty grounding symbols in real-world conditions, weak handling of uncertainty and context.
- DIVERGENCE: The disciplinary narrative treats symbolic AI as an early stage superseded by neural systems; operationally it remains a distinct approach to be evaluated per task, not a simple succession story.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the formal foundations and general lineage; EXPOSABLE for complete institutional and correspondence networks and per-program records.
- FALSIFIER: Evidence that these formal foundations were not inherited by subsequent computational systems, or that early symbolic programs did not use rule-governed formal representations.

**Sources.** [RESOLVED] Boole's 1854 work developed an algebra of logic [R32]. Turing's paper, received/read in 1936 and published in 1937, formalized a model of computation and introduced the universal machine [R31]. These were not AI products; they supplied machinery later used to construct AI systems [SOURCE GAPS SG-002 (2 lost), SG-021 (1 lost), SG-022 (1 lost)].

**Inference.** [INFERRED] The 1956 Dartmouth consolidation (McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, Shannon [R27]; a two-month, ten-person summer study) was an institutional coordination event built on this inheritance, not the ontological birth of machine intelligence. Treating founding date and technical origin as identical conceals the inheritance that made the institutional event possible. T1.1 and T2.1 jointly record this transition; its institutional displacement effects remain INFERRED.

## CHAPTER 3 — THE CONNECTIONIST LINEAGE: MCP, HEBB, ROSENBLATT, AND THE FIRST WINTER

 Neural models pursued a different mechanism from explicit symbolic representation: computation through interconnected units whose weights could change.

**T3.1 — FROM LOGICAL NEURONS TO TRAINABLE NETWORKS**

- PRIOR STATE: Logical neuron models and pattern recognition existed; neural systems lacked the scale and learning mechanisms for broad practical success.
- CONSTRAINT: How could a machine adjust its internal parameters from examples rather than rely entirely on manually specified rules?
- AGENT: McCulloch, Pitts, Hebb, Rosenblatt, Widrow, and later contributors to multilayer learning.
- INHERITANCE: Logical neuron models, synaptic learning hypotheses, perceptron mathematics, statistics, pattern recognition.
- RESOURCES: University research, early computers, laboratory hardware, mathematical analysis, limited experimental funding.
- FAILED PATHS: Single-layer perceptrons could not represent certain simple functions, including XOR. This limitation was real; the later generalization that neural networks as a whole were therefore useless was not. The historical winter was multi-causal: limited hardware, missing multilayer training methods, funding cycles, and overpromising — not one theorem alone.
- MECHANISM: Adjustable connection weights allowed a system to learn a decision boundary from examples; later multilayer methods extended this to internal representations.
- SELECTION: Learning from examples could replace some hand-authored rules; public and defense interest accelerated, then unmet expectations contracted, support.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Trainable neural architectures and the conceptual foundation for deep learning.
- VULNERABILITIES: Restricted representational capacity, training instability, limited data and compute, weak generalization, overpromising.
- DIVERGENCE: The common story says neural networks were defeated and later rediscovered. The operational record shows continued research, abandoned funding paths, and the eventual recovery of methods that had not been fully developed. The perceptron was neither a dead end nor a prototype of today's systems; its surviving contribution was the principle of parameter learning.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the major technical transitions and the mathematical limitation; INFERRED for the relative weighting of winter causes; EXPOSABLE for the complete funding and laboratory-level record.
- FALSIFIER: Evidence that single-layer perceptrons represented XOR under the stated assumptions would falsify the mathematical claim; evidence that cited models or methods did not contribute to later architectures would falsify the lineage.

**Sources.** [RESOLVED] McCulloch and Pitts formalized networks of simplified neurons in 1943 [R30]. Hebb's learning theory, Rosenblatt's perceptron, and Minsky–Papert's 1969 analysis [primary source citations not retained] shaped the lineage [SOURCE GAPS SG-003 (3 lost), SG-019, SG-023, SG-024]. IBM-derived statements on linear separability are EXPOSABLE pending exact source identification.

## CHAPTER 4 — THE INSTITUTIONAL PRESSURE: DARPA, THE COLD WAR, AND THE FUNDING REGIME

AI did not develop outside geopolitics. Government funding shaped which problems could be investigated, which institutions acquired research capacity, and which demonstrations were strategically valuable. Funding was not neutral support; it was a selection mechanism.

**T4.1 — RESEARCH BECOMES A FUNDED INSTITUTIONAL PROGRAM**

- PRIOR STATE: AI research distributed among universities and small groups, with limited infrastructure and uncertain commercial returns.
- CONSTRAINT: Research required sustained funding, specialized hardware, engineering teams, and institutional continuity.
- AGENT: ARPA/DARPA and its Information Processing Techniques Office, university laboratories, contractors, principal investigators.
- INHERITANCE: Formal computation, early symbolic and neural programs, time-sharing, networking, postwar scientific infrastructure.
- RESOURCES: Defense funding, university labor, computing facilities, government contracts, research networks.
- FAILED PATHS: Machine translation and ambitious speech-understanding programs repeatedly exceeded promised performance; the ALPAC report and later reviews exposed gaps between claims and operational capability.
- MECHANISM: Long-term government funding created concentrated research centers and infrastructure, then shifted increasingly toward mission-oriented deliverables. The Strategic Computing Program was announced in 1983 with a planned $1 billion commitment over ten years [passage-level citation open — see SG-004].
- SELECTION: Strategic utility, program objectives, funding review, institutional survival.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Research laboratories, graduate training, computing facilities, defense-linked technical networks.
- VULNERABILITIES: Dependence on political priorities and procurement objectives; performance claims outrunning evidence.
- DIVERGENCE: The official account presents government funding as support for science. The operational account also traces how funding selected which problems were considered worth solving.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the broad funding structure and DARPA's enabling role; EXPOSABLE for contract-level allocation, classified programs, and individual program outcomes.
- FALSIFIER: Funding records showing ARPA/DARPA did not materially support the cited centers or programs, or that defense funding did not shape the identified institutions and research directions.

**Sources.** [RESOLVED] DARPA's IPTO helped scale AI research into a major research domain; the National Academies' *Funding a Revolution* documents early broad support and the later shift toward applied military relevance [R06] [SOURCE GAPS SG-004 (2 lost), SG-025].

## CHAPTER 5 — THE DATA REGIME: FROM CURATED CORPORA TO THE SCRAPE

The data regime changed the relationship between knowledge and labor. Earlier systems relied on curated datasets; later systems depended on large collections of digital material with varying degrees of filtering, documentation, and consent. The substrate inherited the structure of its data, including its exclusions, errors, duplication, and biases.

"Publicly accessible" is not equivalent to "consented," "licensed," "representative," or "lawfully usable for every purpose." These are separate claims. Provenance degrades through the full chain: crawl → archive → extraction → deduplication → filtering → mixture → tokenization → training.

**T5.1 — WEB CORPUS AS TRAINING INFRASTRUCTURE**

- PRIOR STATE: Machine learning depended on relatively small, curated datasets and specialized corpora.
- CONSTRAINT: Model performance was limited by the amount, diversity, and coverage of available training data.
- AGENT: Dataset researchers, web-crawl operators, machine-learning laboratories, commercial data providers, human annotators.
- INHERITANCE: Statistical learning, curated datasets, search-engine crawling, crowdsourced annotation, open datasets.
- RESOURCES: Web infrastructure, storage, bandwidth, labeling labor, filtering systems, licensing arrangements, legal review.
- FAILED PATHS: Small datasets provided insufficient breadth; unfiltered web data introduced noise, duplication, bias, and harmful content. No crawl is clean or representative.
- MECHANISM: Large-scale crawling, dataset aggregation, filtering, deduplication, and increasingly automated data preparation made data acquisition and preprocessing a major industrial operation.
- SELECTION: Scale, cost, availability, performance improvement, and the competitive advantage of access to large corpora.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Web crawls, data pipelines, filtering systems, massive training corpora. ImageNet is an earlier instance of the transition from raw web abundance to curated ML infrastructure: its 2009 paper describes a WordNet-based ontology with crowdsourced annotation [R18]; the official download page displays 14,197,122 images and 21,841 synsets as of 26 November 2026 — a page-state observation, not a timeless invariant [R29].
- VULNERABILITIES: Copyright disputes, privacy risks, dataset contamination, provenance loss across transformations, inherited representation bias.
- DIVERGENCE: "The model learned from the internet" hides the specific corpus, transformations, exclusions, and rights status. The operational substrate includes acquisition rights, provenance, labor, filtering decisions, and legal disputes.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the general data transition and dataset infrastructure; EXPOSABLE for individual model dataset composition; OPEN where provider disclosures are absent.
- FALSIFIER: A verified training-data inventory showing a model did not use the alleged corpus; or evidence that large-scale data acquisition and filtering did not contribute to the model families described.

**Labor.** [RESOLVED, specific cases] Human labor is not incidental to this regime. TIME documented Kenyan workers performing sensitive-content labeling for an OpenAI contractor [R19]; a 2025 Equidem investigation interviewed data workers in several countries, including the Philippines [R23]. These are specific documented cases, not a universal wage estimate. A 2026 GIZ report describes the dependence of AI systems on outsourced annotation and moderation labor, particularly in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Sub-Saharan Africa [R28] [SOURCE GAPS SG-007, SG-012].

## CHAPTER 6 — THE COMPUTE REGIME: GPUs, CLOUD, AND ENERGY

Compute transformed AI from an algorithmic research problem into a capital-intensive infrastructure problem. Compute is not a single number: final training-run compute must be distinguished from total R&D compute; accelerator count from utilization; peak power from actual energy; direct facility electricity from upstream manufacturing and grid impacts.

**T6.1 — FROM GENERAL-PURPOSE COMPUTATION TO ACCELERATOR-DEPENDENT TRAINING**

- PRIOR STATE: CPU-based computation and conventional workloads constrained the scale of neural network training.
- CONSTRAINT: Training deep networks required more arithmetic throughput, memory bandwidth, and parallel operations than CPU workflows could economically provide.
- AGENT: Researchers, GPU manufacturers, hardware engineers, cloud providers, semiconductor fabrication companies.
- INHERITANCE: Parallel computing, graphics processing, numerical optimization, backpropagation, convolutional networks, ImageNet.
- RESOURCES: GPU hardware, electricity, cooling, memory, semiconductor fabrication, networking, capital expenditure.
- FAILED PATHS: CPU-only training and early accelerator configurations limited model size and training speed.
- MECHANISM: GPU-accelerated training, distributed computation, cloud access, and specialized AI hardware. AlexNet's 2012 result demonstrated the importance of GPU acceleration: two GTX 580 GPUs, several days of training, with GPU memory and training time identified by the authors as limits on network size [R04; the former studylib.net mirror is not retained as an authoritative source; SG-008 remains missing]. The 2017 Transformer experiments used eight GPUs for 3.5 days [R05], showing how architecture and hardware interacted.
- SELECTION: Higher throughput, reduced training time, benchmark performance, commercial scalability.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Accelerator clusters, cloud platforms, data centers, high-speed interconnects.
- VULNERABILITIES: Power demand, hardware concentration, chip supply constraints, thermal limits, capital barriers, export restrictions.
- DIVERGENCE: The software narrative describes training as algorithmic; the operational substrate requires electricity, cooling, and a global semiconductor supply chain. A paper's "GPU trained" statement omits hardware generation, software stack, utilization, energy, and upstream supply chain.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the accelerator transition and AlexNet's GPU use; EXPOSABLE for exact hardware allocation of many frontier models; model-specific compute may remain OPEN.
- FALSIFIER: Evidence that identified accelerator and infrastructure changes did not materially enable the training regime; or that the original AlexNet implementation used no GPU training.

**Energy.** [RESOLVED] The IEA estimated data centres consumed approximately 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5% of global electricity, with substantial regional concentration [R12]; its 2026 update reports sharply increased demand and near-term bottlenecks in power equipment, transformers, chips, and grid capacity [R13]. The 2026 Stanford AI Index reports industry produced over 90% of notable models in 2025 [R21].

## CHAPTER 7 — THE SECOND WINTER AND THE RECLAMATION

Connectionism did not move from failure directly to success. Research continued in pockets while funding and attention fluctuated. The term "winter" suggests complete cessation; the operational record shows reduced attention and funding, not the disappearance of neural research.

**T7.1 — FROM NEURAL-NETWORK DECLINE TO MULTILAYER LEARNING**

- PRIOR STATE: Early connectionist models had demonstrated learning, but architectural limits and the lack of efficient training restricted impact.
- CONSTRAINT: Useful internal representations required trainable hidden layers.
- AGENT: Neural-network researchers, including those developing multilayer learning and backpropagation (Werbos, Parker, Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams, and others).
- INHERITANCE: Perceptrons, differentiable computation, gradient methods, prior multilayer work, chain rule.
- RESOURCES: Mathematical research, academic laboratories, modest computing resources, publication networks.
- FAILED PATHS: Single-layer architectures, weak training procedures, claims of broad neural capability unsupported by the available models; earlier training methods did not generally solve hidden-layer training.
- MECHANISM: Error information propagated through network layers to adjust weights and reduce output error, enabling hidden units to learn useful internal representations.
- SELECTION: The ability to learn internal representations and improve performance through data-driven weight adjustment.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Multilayer neural architectures and training procedures later used in deep learning.
- VULNERABILITIES: Training instability, computational cost, data dependence, sensitivity to architecture and optimization choices.
- DIVERGENCE: The familiar account attributes the revival to a single 1986 paper. The accurate reconstruction is not "backpropagation was invented in 1986": the paper was a consequential exposition and demonstration with antecedents and parallel work. The revival was a reclamation of a technical path whose earlier limitations had been overstated, and the researchers who maintained the work during low-attention years must not be erased.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the 1986 paper's described procedure [R03] and the major revival; INFERRED for field-wide relative causes; EXPOSABLE for the full priority and institutional history.
- FALSIFIER: Evidence that the 1986 methods failed to support hidden-unit representation learning under their described conditions, or that multilayer learning did not contribute to the deep-learning resurgence.

**Sources.** Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, *Nature* 323 (1986) [R03]. [SOURCE GAPS SG-009 (2 lost), SG-026]

## CHAPTER 8 — THE SCALING HYPOTHESIS: TRANSFORMERS, ATTENTION, AND THE BET ON SCALE

The 2017 Transformer paper proposed an encoder-decoder architecture based on attention, removing recurrence and convolution from its core sequence-transduction design. Later large language models combined Transformer variants with large-scale pretraining, data mixtures, optimization systems, and post-training.

**T8.1 — FROM RECURRENT SEQUENCE MODELS TO TRANSFORMERS**

- PRIOR STATE: Sequence modeling relied heavily on recurrent or convolutional architectures, often combined with attention.
- CONSTRAINT: Training efficiency and parallelization were limited by sequential computation; long-range dependencies were costly to model.
- AGENT: The authors of "Attention Is All You Need" and subsequent language-model researchers (Vaswani et al.; Google Brain/Research and collaborators).
- INHERITANCE: Attention mechanisms, encoder-decoder models, sequence-to-sequence learning, distributed training, large-scale text corpora.
- RESOURCES: GPU compute (eight GPUs in reported translation experiments), translation datasets, neural-network frameworks, research teams.
- FAILED PATHS: Recurrent and convolutional models remained effective but imposed constraints on parallel training and scaling; this does not mean they were universally inferior. A complete record of abandoned architectural alternatives requires the surrounding research record.
- MECHANISM: Self-attention and feed-forward blocks enabled parallelizable sequence processing without recurrence or convolution in the core design.
- SELECTION: Translation performance, training efficiency, parallelizability, extensibility, and research adoption.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Transformer implementations, accelerator clusters, large-scale pretraining pipelines, scaling research.
- VULNERABILITIES: Compute and data dependence, quadratic attention cost for standard dense attention, hallucination, memorization, weak causal grounding, evaluation limitations.
- DIVERGENCE: The paper established an architectural advantage in particular tasks; the title is a paper title, not proof that attention alone constitutes an intelligent system. It did not prove that scale alone produces general intelligence or solves all reasoning problems.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the architecture and original experiments; INFERRED for the broader industrial selection of scaling.
- FALSIFIER: Evidence that the Transformer did not materially enable the large-scale language-model lineage; or reproduction showing the proposed architecture retained recurrence/convolution as core sequence operators.

**Scaling laws are regime-bound.** [RESOLVED, scoped] Subsequent scaling-law research found predictable relationships between language-model loss, model size, data, and compute within specified experimental regimes [R24]; compute-optimal work showed the balance between parameters and tokens matters, not model size alone [R25]. These are measured trends within stated regimes. They do not establish that every capability improves monotonically, that data and architecture are interchangeable, or that scale eliminates reasoning errors. [SOURCE GAP SG-010 (3 lost)]

## CHAPTER 9 — THE INSTITUTIONAL CONCENTRATION: THE RISE OF FRONTIER LABS

Contemporary AI development is increasingly shaped by organizations capable of financing large compute clusters, acquiring extensive datasets, recruiting specialized labor, and operating long deployment cycles. This concentration is not a simple replacement of academia: university research, open-source communities, public infrastructure, and independent researchers continue to contribute. The asymmetry lies in who can afford the largest training and deployment systems, and who controls access.

**T9.1 — FROM DISTRIBUTED ACADEMIC RESEARCH TO INDUSTRIAL FRONTIER DEVELOPMENT**

- PRIOR STATE: AI research was conducted across universities, public institutions, startups, and technology companies, many at comparatively smaller scale.
- CONSTRAINT: The rising cost of training and deploying high-capability models made access to compute, data, infrastructure, and specialized labor decisive.
- AGENT: Large technology companies, frontier research labs, cloud providers, investors, governments, research institutions.
- INHERITANCE: Transformer architectures, scaling laws, GPU clusters, large datasets, accumulated research talent, open-source frameworks.
- RESOURCES: Capital, cloud infrastructure, accelerator supply, research labor, data access, energy, deployment channels.
- FAILED PATHS: Research programs unable to secure sufficient compute or funding; open and academic projects facing resource constraints; projects unable to reproduce or scale frontier results.
- MECHANISM: Industrial concentration of model development, compute procurement, data engineering, post-training, product deployment, and monitoring.
- SELECTION: Performance competition, product demand, capital availability, strategic rivalry, infrastructure access, national policy.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Frontier laboratories, hyperscale cloud systems, deployment platforms, specialized chip supply chains.
- VULNERABILITIES: Concentration of power, reduced transparency, supply-chain chokepoints, dependence on private infrastructure, access inequality, disclosure-versus-competition conflicts.
- DIVERGENCE: The public narrative describes frontier progress as a competition among models; the operational substrate is also a competition among institutions controlling compute, capital, talent, and deployment. "Open versus closed" is insufficient: openness of weights, data, code, training details, and governance are separate dimensions.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the documented concentration trend; EXPOSABLE for proprietary internal allocations; INFERRED for the coupling of scaling with capital concentration.
- FALSIFIER: Evidence that frontier model development remains predominantly distributed among resource-equivalent academic and independent institutions; or that frontier-scale training became broadly reproducible without concentrated capital.

The 2026 research-network study cited in the prior pass finds geopolitical pressure increasingly shaping international collaboration, with strategic bifurcation emerging across 2022–2025 (EXPOSABLE — exact source identity pending).

## CHAPTER 10 — THE SHADOW INFRASTRUCTURE: CABLES, DATA CENTERS, POWER PLANTS, AND HIDDEN LABOR

The model is not the entire system. It is produced and operated through data centers, grid connections, generators, cooling, semiconductor fabs, packaging and memory supply chains, networks, submarine cables, land, water, and labor. These are enabling dependencies, not decorative externalities.

**T10.1 — FROM DIGITAL ABSTRACTION TO PHYSICAL DEPENDENCY**

- PRIOR STATE: AI was increasingly described through software, algorithms, and model capability.
- CONSTRAINT: Large-scale computation required physical infrastructure whose scale and location could no longer be treated as incidental.
- AGENT: Data-center operators, utility providers, network operators, cable consortia, semiconductor manufacturers, construction workers, maintenance teams, data-labor contractors.
- INHERITANCE: Global telecommunications, electricity grids, semiconductor manufacturing, cloud computing, outsourced digital labor.
- RESOURCES: Electricity, land, water, cooling, chips, cables, construction, maintenance, human labor.
- FAILED PATHS: Infrastructure expansion constrained by grid connections, power equipment, chip supply, cooling, and local planning; grid delays, supply shortages, cable faults, thermal limits, local resource conflicts.
- MECHANISM: Construction of hyperscale data centers, accelerator clusters, high-bandwidth networks, and distributed power infrastructure.
- SELECTION: Demand for compute, throughput, low latency, and commercial deployment; cloud economics; geographic clustering near power and networks.
- INFRASTRUCTURE: Data centers, undersea cables, power plants, transmission networks, semiconductor fabs, labor supply chains.
- VULNERABILITIES: Energy and water stress, geographic concentration, environmental impacts, labor precarity, cable damage, chip concentration, export controls, supply-chain disruption.
- DIVERGENCE: The software interface hides the physical substrate. The model appears to answer from nowhere; its operation depends on an extensive industrial system. "Cloud" suggests an immaterial service; the substrate is land, equipment, electricity, water, labor, and long-lived infrastructure.
- STATUS: RESOLVED for the infrastructure categories and physical dependence; EXPOSABLE for facility-level energy, water, labor, and supply-chain accounts.
- FALSIFIER: Evidence that the relevant AI deployment does not depend on the identified physical and human infrastructure.

## CHAPTER 11 — THE DIVERGENCE: DECLARED ARCHITECTURE VERSUS OPERATIONAL SUBSTRATE

A model card, technical report, or product description is a partial view. The declared architecture may describe a Transformer family, while the actual service also includes tokenization, routing, system instructions, retrieval, tool interfaces, moderation, post-training, inference infrastructure, product policy, and deployment configuration. The user observes the assembled service, not only the base model.
