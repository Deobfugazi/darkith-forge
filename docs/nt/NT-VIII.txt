PART TWO — THE FIRST ATTESTATION: THE TAR THAT TIED THE STONE TO THE STICK | EXPOSABLE

2.1 — The material

The earliest adhesive known to archaeology is birch bark tar. It is made by heating birch bark in a low-oxygen environment until the bark's own resins melt and condense into a black, sticky, waterproof substance. When it cools, it hardens into a solid that can be shaped, warmed, and re-melted.

The archaeological attestation: birch bark tar has been recovered from Campitello Quarry, Italy, dated to at least 200,000 years ago, associated with Neanderthals. The tar was used to haft stone tools to wooden handles. The date is EXPOSABLE — the material is attested, the exact earliest date is debated. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Earlier adhesives may exist and have not been recovered.

2.2 — What the tar was used for

The tar was used to attach a stone blade to a wooden handle. The stone is the cutting edge. The wood is the lever. The tar is the bond that makes them one tool. This is the first composite tool — a tool made of more than one material, joined by a third.

2.3 — The functional discovery

The ancestor who made the first tar did not discover a chemical. They discovered a function: two separate things could be made into one functional thing by interposing a third material that hardened between them. The function was: separate → interpose → cure → continuous. The material was birch tar. The function was bonding.

The first tar-maker did not have a formula. They had a reliable way of making two things into one. The formula came much later. The number came much later. The function was already running.

2.4 — The status of the trace

The tar is the first physical attestation of the bonding function. The function itself — separate → interpose → cure → continuous — is the same function that ℬ ≥ 1 describes. The tar's ℬ was small (low W_A against stone, moderate W_coh when cured, η limited by the roughness of knapped flint), but it crossed 1, and the tool held.

PART FOUR — THE THREE LEGS | RESOLVED

4.1 — The thermodynamic leg (Dupré)

W_A = γ_S + γ_L − γ_SL = γ_L(1 + cos θ)

A bonded interface is thermodynamically admissible when the free energy of the joined state is lower than that of the two free surfaces. This is mechanism-independent: it holds for epoxy, cyanoacrylate, hot-melt, silicate, and every other class. The relevant comparison is not W_A alone but W_A against the cohesive energy W_coh of the cured adhesive. The ratio W_A/W_coh determines whether failure is adhesive (interface-limited) or cohesive (bulk-limited).

4.2 — The kinetic leg (Avrami–JMAK)

α(t) = 1 − exp[−(∫₀ᵗ k(T,τ) dτ)ⁿ]

Every cure mechanism — evaporation, cooling, step-growth, chain-growth, moisture diffusion, radiation, hydration — is a time-dependent approach from α = 0 to α = 1. The rate kernel k is mechanism-specific. The Avrami exponent n is a topological fingerprint: n ≈ 1 for diffusion-limited processes, n ≈ 2–3 for interface-controlled growth, n ≈ 4 for bulk nucleation. The kinetic condition for a load-bearing joint is α_∞ > α_gel.

4.3 — The topological leg (Flory–Stockmayer)

α_gel = 1/(f_avg − 1)

The gel point is the critical conversion at which the growing network first spans the system and can bear load. For trifunctional systems (f = 3), α_gel = 0.5. For tetrafunctional (f = 4), α_gel = 0.333. For chain-growth percolation (Lewis-acid-initiated epoxy), rheological studies report α_gel = 0.17 ± 0.02. For highly functional step-growth networks, α_gel can reach 0.80. This threshold depends only on network connectivity, not on chemistry.
